\title{Publicly Verifiable Generalized Secret Sharing Schemes and Their Applications}

\begin{abstract}
% Generalized secret sharing (GSS) enables flexible access control in distributed systems by allowing secrets to be shared across arbitrary monotone access structures.
% However, its adoption in transparent and trustless environments is hindered due to the reliance on trusted participants and secure communication channels.
% This reliance restricts GSS's ability to provide flexible control in the presence of adversaries.
% In this paper, we propose publicly verifiable generalized secret sharing (PVGSS), a scheme that allows to distribute a secret using generalized access structures while enabling non-interactive public verifiability of participant honesty.
% PVGSS scheme offers significant potential to advance the fields of blockchain and applied cryptography, such as attribute-based cryptosystem and on-chain secret escrow.
% Intuitively, we first build two GSS schemes by leveraging recursive Shamir secret sharing and linear secret sharing scheme.
% Then, we encrypt GSS shares and generate the corresponding non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs. Furthermore, we innovatively apply the GSS reconstruction algorithm to show all encrypted shares bind to the dealer's secret, with only \( O(|\mathbb{A}|) \) verification complexity, where \( |\mathbb{A}| \) is the number of leaf nodes in the access structure \( \mathbb{A} \).
% To exemplify the practical applicability, we implement a decentralized exchange (DEX) protocol, where fairness and accountable arbitration are considered.
% Our benchmarks on the BN128 curve demonstrate the computational efficiency of PVGSS schemes, while Ethereum gas cost analysis confirms the viability of the DEX implementation.
Secret sharing under expressive access structures and public verifiability have largely been developed in separate lines of work. Generalized secret sharing (GSS) supports arbitrary monotone access structures, but relies on private channels and gives no public evidence that the dealer distributed a consistent sharing. Publicly verifiable secret sharing (PVSS), in contrast, supports public verification over insecure channels, but is fundamentally tailored to threshold policies. We introduce publicly verifiable generalized secret sharing (PVGSS), a primitive that combines arbitrary monotone access structures with non-interactive public verification of both encrypted shares and decrypted reconstruction shares. Our construction encrypts only the leaf shares of an underlying GSS instance and proves, via Schnorr-type NIZK proofs, that all ciphertexts are globally consistent with a single secret. The key technical ingredient is a GSS testing procedure that checks this global consistency from proof responses, without revealing the shares. We instantiate PVGSS from recursive Shamir secret sharing and from linear secret sharing schemes, obtaining verification complexity linear in the number of leaves of the access structure. We formalize correctness, encrypted-share verifiability, share-decryption verifiability, and IND1-secrecy, and prove security in the random oracle model under DDH, knowledge soundness of the proof systems, and privacy of the underlying GSS-on-group sharing. As an application, we show how PVGSS enables publicly accountable secret release in a decentralized exchange protocol.

\keywords{Access structure, Public verifiability, PVGSS, GSS Testing, Decentralized exchange}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

On the one hand, one-to-many data sharing with flexible access policies allows secret reconstruction by diverse and dynamic subsets of participants. On the other hand, detecting faulty behaviors throughout the entire data lifecycle is essential for safeguarding data sovereignty and enforcing privilege boundaries. Against this backdrop, the design of publicly verifiable generalized secret sharing (PVGSS) schemes emerges as a particularly compelling direction.

Secret sharing (SS)~\cite{Shamir79SS} is a basic mechanism for distributing trust: a dealer encodes a secret into shares so that only authorized subsets of parties can reconstruct it. Verifiable secret sharing (VSS)~\cite{Feldman1987VSS,Bagherpour2022VPSS,Alon1995VSS} strengthens this primitive by making dealer misbehavior detectable, and publicly verifiable secret sharing (PVSS)~\cite{Stadler1996PVSS,Cascudo2017PVSS,cascudo2024publicly} further allows external observers to verify encrypted shares over public channels. This public-verification feature is essential in settings where there is no trusted delivery channel and where correctness must be checked without interaction.

The limitation is that public verifiability has mostly been developed for threshold access structures. Threshold policies are useful, but they cannot naturally express hierarchical, weighted, compartmented, or composite authorization requirements. Generalized secret sharing (GSS)~\cite{Ito1987GSS,benaloh1990generalized,Beimel2025GSS} addresses this expressiveness problem by supporting arbitrary monotone access structures, and linear secret sharing schemes (LSSS)~\cite{Karchmer1993LSSS,Beimel2011LSSS,Beimel2025GSS} underlie many expressive access-control mechanisms, including ciphertext-policy attribute-based encryption (CPABE)~\cite{Bethencourt2007CPABE,Lewko2011DABE}. Classical GSS and LSSS, however, assume private share delivery and do not provide a public certificate that the dealer generated a globally consistent sharing. Thus, existing primitives leave a gap between expressiveness and public accountability.

This gap is not closed by composing existing PVSS schemes. In a generalized access structure, the shares associated with the leaves are not independent threshold sharings of the same secret; rather, they are recursively or linearly related through the whole access policy. A verifier must therefore check a global statement: the encrypted leaf shares must be encryptions of shares that reconstruct to one common root secret under the specified access structure. Proving only local well-formedness of each ciphertext is insufficient, while revealing the shares would destroy secrecy.

We introduce publicly verifiable generalized secret sharing (PVGSS) to capture this goal. A PVGSS scheme distributes a secret according to an arbitrary monotone access structure, publishes encrypted leaf shares, and provides non-interactive proofs that any party can verify. The dealer proves that the encrypted shares are globally consistent with a single secret; later, each shareholder proves that its decrypted reconstruction share is correct. When PVGSS is used inside a larger protocol, application-specific identifiers can be included by that protocol to prevent cross-session replay.

Our construction starts from a GSS instance, either recursive Shamir secret sharing or an LSSS-based sharing. The dealer encrypts each leaf share as \(C_i=\pk_i^{s_i}\) and produces Schnorr-type Fiat-Shamir NIZK proofs for the encrypted shares. The main technical step is a \emph{GSS testing} procedure: using only the NIZK response values, it checks that the hidden shares satisfy the same generalized access structure and bind to the same dealer secret. For reconstruction, shareholders output group shares together with DLEQ proofs, allowing public verification of decryption correctness. % Achieving PVGSS is non-trivial: a PVGSS construction cannot be obtained by a naïve composition of PVSS instances, as this would violate correctness under generalized access structures.
Crucially, PVGSS cannot be obtained by naively combining existing PVSS schemes. The core technical challenge of PVGSS is to prove, in a publicly verifiable manner, that a collection of encrypted shares \(\{C_i\}\) is globally consistent with a single underlying secret under an arbitrary access structure. Suppose the access structure is represented as a tree, the secret is associated with the root while encrypted shares are assigned only to the leaf nodes, rendering any straightforward combination of multiple PVSS instances ineffective.
To overcome this challenge, we build PVGSS schemes atop the GSS framework, instantiated using either recursive Shamir secret sharing or linear secret sharing schemes (LSSS). Each GSS share \(s_i\) is encrypted under the public key of shareholder \(P_i\) and is accompanied by Schnorr-type non‑interactive zero‑knowledge (NIZK) proofs derived via the Fiat–Shamir heuristic~\cite{fiat1986prove}.
Furthermore, we propose a verification algorithm (i.e., GSS testing) to enable validity checks for all shares over an arbitrary access structure. With this algorithm, we establish a binding relation between all encrypted shares and the dealer’s secret, thereby addressing the challenge of public verifiability in secret sharing over general access structures.
% Importantly, the verification complexity remains linear in the number of leaf nodes of the access structure \(\mathbb{A}\), i.e., \(\mathcal{O}(|\mathbb{A}|)\).







\subsection{Applications}
\label{sec:other_applications}
PVGSS introduces a cryptographic primitive that simultaneously supports expressive access control and non-interactive public accountability—two properties that rarely coexist in prior systems. This combination enables new capabilities across several security-critical domains where correctness must be verifiable by untrusted observers and where access policies extend far beyond simple thresholds.
In Section~\ref{sec:dex_on_pvgss}, we will dive deep into a decentralized exchange (DEX) protocol applying PVGSS.


\paragraph{Large-scale beacon protocols.}
Existing PVSS-based distributed randomness beacons~\cite{Choi2023Beacon} protocols are fundamentally constrained by threshold policies, limiting committee flexibility and preventing hierarchical or weighted participation. PVGSS enables publicly verifiable share consistency under arbitrary monotone structures, allowing beacon protocols to incorporate multi-tier committees, heterogeneous trust assumptions, and application-specific quorum rules without sacrificing transparency. This expands the design space for scalable, decentralized randomness generation.
For example, by partitioning $n$ distributed parties into subgroups of size $c$, one can define an access control policy of the form
$(d \text{ of } (\frac{c}{3} \text{ of } P_1,\ldots,P_c), (\frac{c}{3} \text{ of } P_{c+1}$, $\ldots,P_{2c}), \ldots, (\frac{c}{3} \text{ of } P_{(\frac{n}{c}-1)c+1},\ldots,P_n))$, where the parameter $d$ is introduced to balance unpredictability and liveness of the beacon~\cite{Syta2017Beacon,Schindler2020Beacon}. Under this policy, each party communicates only with a small subgroup of peers, thereby improving scalability.


\paragraph{Attribute-based cryptosystem.} PVGSS can enhance attribute-based cryptosystems~\cite{Bethencourt2007CPABE,Lewko2011DABE} by adding public accountability to key distribution and access-control related computations. In particular, PVGSS can be used as an auditable sharing layer for systems that already rely on LSSS-style access policies. Moreover, PVGSS enables CPABE encryptors to outsource encryption to untrusted servers while ensuring public accountability for their behaviors.




\paragraph{Blockchain ecosystems.}
Blockchain environments require protocols that operate over public channels, tolerate adversarial participants, and provide transparent correctness guarantees. PVGSS naturally applies to the scenarios below:

$\bullet$ PVGSS, functioning as a timed commitment~\cite{tyagi2023riggs}, can be effectively used in on-chain \textbf{secret escrow} systems~\cite{Benhamouda2020Secret,Goyal2022Secret,Cerulli2023Secret} to provide fine-grained and verifiable management of witnesses, thereby facilitating practical deployments of e‑voting and auction protocols.

$\bullet$ In blockchain \textbf{bridges}~\cite{Xie2022zkBridge}, PVGSS ensures that the secret values used to lock and release assets are verifiably shared, thereby coordinating data synchronization across multiple validators or blockchains under a single access control policy.

$\bullet$ In \textbf{relay networks}~\cite{Li2022Bridge}, PVGSS enables secure forwarding of state proofs with public verifiability among different groups or committees.

$\bullet$ In payment or state \textbf{channels}~\cite{Dziembowski2018Channel}, PVGSS supports flexible quorum policies for dispute resolution, allowing participants to reconstruct secrets only under authorized conditions.

$\bullet$ Incorporating PVGSS into \textbf{oracle}~\cite{Park2023Oracle} design strengthens functionality by ensuring decentralized, publicly verifiable data feeds and enabling flexible privilege weighting among oracle nodes.

$\bullet$ By designing robust access control structures, KYC/AML (i.e., the compliance measurements of know your customer/ anti money laundering) can be effectively enforced as required by \textbf{stablecoin governance}. For example, an access policy such as  \((3 \text{ of } (Alice,\) \(Bob, (t \text{ of } Reg_1,\cdots, Reg_n)))\) enables a threshold number of regulators to transparently monitor the transaction between exchangers (i.e., Alice and Bob).

\subsection{Contributions}

$\bullet$ \textbf{PVGSS as an expressive and publicly verifiable primitive.}
We formalize Publicly Verifiable Generalized Secret Sharing (PVGSS), which combines arbitrary monotone access structures with non-interactive public verification over insecure channels. The syntax captures setup, sharing, public verification of encrypted shares, publicly verifiable share decryption, and reconstruction. The security model covers correctness, encrypted-share verifiability, share-decryption verifiability, and IND1-secrecy for unauthorized labelled sets.

$\bullet$ \textbf{Constructions via GSS testing.}
We build PVGSS from GSS-on-group sharing and instantiate it with two representative generalized sharing mechanisms: recursive Shamir secret sharing and LSSS-based sharing. The dealer encrypts only the leaf shares and attaches Schnorr-type Fiat-Shamir NIZK proofs. The key technical component is \emph{GSS testing}, which uses the proof responses to check global consistency under the declared access structure without revealing the shares. We give both exact-interpolation and dual-code testing variants, and the public verification uses a linear number of group operations in the number of leaves.

$\bullet$ \textbf{Security analysis.}
We prove the constructions secure in the random oracle model under DDH, knowledge soundness of the Fiat-Shamir proofs, DLEQ soundness, and privacy of the underlying GSS-on-group scheme. Encrypted-share verifiability follows from extraction and GSS-testing soundness; share-decryption verifiability reduces to DLEQ soundness; and IND1-secrecy is established through hybrids that simulate proofs and hide unauthorized shares.

$\bullet$ \textbf{A DEX application with implementation evidence.}
We apply PVGSS to a decentralized exchange protocol in which exchangers commit to secrets and passive watchers provide accountable arbitration only when disputes arise. We define the ideal DEX functionality and prove that the real-world contract realizes it in the ledger-hybrid model. Because watcher responses are publicly verifiable and economically accountable, the DEX can set \(t=1\) without assuming an honest-watcher threshold for fairness. We implement the PVGSS algorithms in Go and the DEX verification path in Solidity; the appendix reports off-chain benchmark results and on-chain gas costs.

\subsection{Access Structures in Secret Sharing}

As is well known, traditional SS, VSS, and PVSS  protocols~\cite{Shamir79SS,Feldman1987VSS,Stadler1996PVSS,Cascudo2017PVSS,cascudo2024publicly} are designed around the threshold access structure (TAS).
Benaloh et al.~\cite{benaloh1990generalized} introduced the concept of generalized secret sharing (GSS) based on recursive threshold secret sharing, where generalized access structure (GAS) is inherently implied. Beyond  TAS- or GAS- based schemes, some other protocols have explored specific types of access control mechanisms.
% To unify these protocols, we refer to them as multi-level secret sharing schemes.


In particular, Simmons~\cite{Simmons1988SS} points out that real-world applications require more versatile capabilities for secret sharing.
In weighted secret sharing (WSS)~\cite{beimel2005characterizing,Garg2023MPC}, each participant is assigned a positive weight. Only if the sum of weights of some participants exceeds the predefined threshold value, can the secret be reconstructed.



In multipartite secret sharing (MSS)~\cite{Chen2021MSS,tassa2009multipartite}, an access structure defined over $n$ participants is partitioned into disjoint $m(\ll n)$ compartments, where participants within the same compartment have equal weights. In MSS, each compartment must achieve a specific internal agreement before contributing to the reconstruction of the secret.
Tassa et al.~\cite{tassa2009multipartite} point out that any GAS can be regarded as a multipartite access structure with singleton compartments. Hence, reconstructing the secret in MSS scheme depends only on the number of participants from each compartment, rather than their individual identities. In this way, practical applications can focus on $m$ compartments, where TAS is applied, instead of $n$ individuals. However, participants may be designed in different access structures in a single system, resulting in different divisions of disjoint compartments.




 % Farràs, O., Martí-Farré, J. and Padró, C., 2012. Ideal multipartite secret sharing schemes. Journal of cryptology, 25(3), pp.434-463.


Hierarchical secret sharing (HSS)~\cite{tassa2007hierarchical,Chattopadhyay2024SS}, also referred as multilevel secret sharing~\cite{Simmons1988SS,tassa2007hierarchical}, places participants in disjoint levels according to their importance. HSS can be applied in managing staff in big companies, where employees are assigned to different levels with different privileges.
HSS is categorized into disjunctive and conjunctive types~\cite{Drăgan2016DWSS}. Further, Drăgan et al.~\cite{Drăgan2016DWSS} introduce the distributive weighted threshold secret sharing, where participants at the same level have the same weight, and the threshold values are 1.

Other related works, such as proactive secret sharing~\cite{Bagherpour2022VPSS} and multiple secret sharing~\cite{Lin2018PVMSS}, can be regarded as extensions of SS. Moreover, these concepts can also be incorporated into our GSS and PVGSS schemes.

\subsection{(Publicly) Verifiable Secret Sharing}
Dealer in the SS scheme may distribute invalid shares to deviate from the protocol.
To mitigate this problem, VSS~\cite{Feldman1987VSS,Abraham2023VSS} ensures that participants can verify the validity of the shares distributed by the dealer, while PVSS~\cite{Stadler1996PVSS,Cascudo2017PVSS} extends this by allowing any external observer to verify the integrity of the shares. Notably, Galil and Yung~\cite{Galil1987} combine VSS, partition encryption and NIZK proofs to design a more efficient and fully polynomial scheme known as partitioned VSS.


The first VSS scheme, proposed by Feldman~\cite{Feldman1987VSS}, enhances Shamir secret sharing by adding public commitments to ensure share validity. Shareholders verify their shares against these commitments, ensuring the dealer's honesty without revealing the secret.
Recently, polynomial commitments~\cite{Kate2010VSS,Abraham2023VSS,Zhang2024VSS} are widely adopted to design new VSS schemes. The secret shares of VSS should not be transferred publicly.


Stadler~\cite{Stadler1996PVSS} put forward the first PVSS scheme in 1996. Intuitively, PVSS can be achieved by integrating public key encryption into VSS schemes, allowing secret shares to be delivered and verified on public channels. For example, ElGamal, RSA, and Pailler cryptosystems are utilized in \cite{Stadler1996PVSS,Schoenmakers1999PVSS}, \cite{Fujisaki1998PVSS}, and \cite{Ruiz2005PVSS}, respectively.
Early PVSS schemes require $O(nt)$ verification complexity, where $n$ and $t$ are the amount of shareholders and threshold value, respectively.
In 2017, Cascudo et al.~\cite{Cascudo2017PVSS} introduced SCRAPE PVSS which for the first time achieves $O(n)$ verification complexity. As the core technique, Reed-Solomon code is employed to make all encrypted shares be verified in a linear operation.

Recently, Cascudo et al. successively proposed ALBATROSS~\cite{cascudo2020albatross}, HEPVSS/DHPVSS~\cite{cascudo2022yolo}, and qCLPVSS~\cite{cascudo2024publicly}, which all maintain $O(n)$ verification complexity. ALBATROSS~\cite{cascudo2020albatross} leverages low-degree exponent interpolation to generate Fiat-Shamir NIZK proofs.
HEPVSS\cite{cascudo2022yolo} also uses ElGamal to encrypt the secret share, while DHPVSS\cite{cascudo2022yolo} leverages Shamir secret sharing on group $\GG$ to hide shares.
qCLPVSS~\cite{cascudo2024publicly} enables users to recover the original secret $s\in \ZZ_q$ by leveraging class group, making it applicable in more wider applications, such as distributed key generation~\cite{Das2022DKG,cascudo2024publicly} and MPC~\cite{cascudo2024publicly,cascudo2022yolo} protocols.




\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

In this paper, \( \ZZ_q \) denotes a finite field with prime order \( q \), and \( \GG \) represents a cyclic group of the same order generated by \( g \).


\subsection{Lagrange Interpolation and Reed-Solomon Codes}
\label{sec:prelim_lagrange_rs}
We recall two standard algebraic notions used throughout the paper when reasoning about Shamir-type shares and polynomial consistency.

\begin{definition}[Lagrange interpolation]\label{def:lagrange_interpolation}
Let \(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m\in\ZZ_q^*\) be distinct evaluation points. For an index set \(I\subseteq[m]\), the Lagrange basis polynomial associated with \(i\in I\) is
$\ell_i^I(X)=\prod_{j\in I,\,j\neq i}\frac{X-\alpha_j}{\alpha_i-\alpha_j}.$
Given values \(\{y_i\}_{i\in I}\) with \(|I|=t\), there is a unique polynomial \(f\in\ZZ_q[X]\) of degree less than \(t\) satisfying \(f(\alpha_i)=y_i\) for all \(i\in I\), namely
$f(X)=\sum_{i\in I} y_i\ell_i^I(X).$
In particular, Shamir reconstruction recovers the constant term as
$f(0)=\sum_{i\in I} y_i\ell_i^I(0)
    =\sum_{i\in I} y_i\prod_{j\in I,\,j\neq i}\frac{-\alpha_j}{\alpha_i-\alpha_j}.$
More generally, the same interpolation formula determines \(f(\alpha_k)\) at any other evaluation point \(\alpha_k\). Thus a degree-bounded polynomial is uniquely determined by sufficiently many evaluations, and all other evaluations can be derived from them.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Reed-Solomon code and dual code]\label{def:rs_dual_code}
For distinct evaluation points \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m)\in(\ZZ_q^*)^m\), the \([m,t]\) Reed-Solomon code over \(\ZZ_q\) is
$\mathsf{RS}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(m,t)=\{(f(\alpha_1),\ldots,f(\alpha_m)):\ f\in\ZZ_q[X],\ \deg(f)<t\}.$
Its dual code is
$\mathsf{RS}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(m,t)^\perp=\{\boldsymbol{h}\in\ZZ_q^m:\ \langle \boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{c}\rangle=0
    \text{ for all } \boldsymbol{c}\in\mathsf{RS}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(m,t)\}.$
Consequently, a vector \(\boldsymbol{y}\in\ZZ_q^m\) is a valid Reed-Solomon codeword if and only if
\(\langle \boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=0\) for every
\(\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathsf{RS}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(m,t)^\perp\).
Equivalently, if \(\boldsymbol{y}\notin\mathsf{RS}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(m,t)\), then its syndrome is nonzero, and a uniformly random linear combination of dual parity checks accepts it with probability exactly \(1/q\).

In particular, Shamir share vectors are Reed-Solomon codewords. If the constant term is fixed to a value \(x\), then \(x\) and evaluations \(y_1,\ldots,y_m\) are consistent with a degree-\(<t\) polynomial if and only if there exists \(f\in\ZZ_q[X]\) with \(\deg(f)<t\), \(f(0)=x\), and \(f(\alpha_i)=y_i\) for all \(i\in[m]\). Equivalently, the extended vector \((x,y_1,\ldots,y_m)\) is a Reed-Solomon codeword over the evaluation points \((0,\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_m)\).
\end{definition}


\subsection{Generalized Access Structure (GAS)}

\begin{definition}[GAS~\cite{benaloh1990generalized,Lewko2011DABE}]
\label{lab:access_structure}
Let $\mathcal{P}=\{P_1,\cdots,P_n\}$ be a set of parties. A collection $\mathbb{A}\in 2^\mathcal{P}$ is a generalized access structure (GAS, commonly abbreviated as AS in previous research~\cite{benaloh1990generalized,Lewko2011DABE}), if it meets the following two conditions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Non-triviality: if $B\in\mathbb{A}$, then $|B|\neq 0$.
\item Monotonicity: if $B\in \mathbb{A}$, $B  \subseteq  C$, then $C \in\mathbb{A}$.
\end{itemize}
If $B\in \mathbb{A}$, we say $\mathbb{A}$ is satisfied by $B$ or $B$ is an authorized set; Otherwise, $\mathbb{A}$ is not satisfied by $B$ or $B$ is unauthorized.
\end{definition}

\begin{myCor}\label{cor:as_proposition}
    The boolean or threshold-gates based access structure can be regarded as a proposition, which outputs $\true$ given an authorized set and outputs $\false$ otherwise.\qed
\end{myCor}

\begin{myCor}
    An access structure $\mathbb{A}$ can be regarded as a recursive data structure, meaning that any node $x$ in $\mathbb{A}$ with its children nodes is also an access structure, denoted by $\mathbb{A}_x$. \end{myCor}

\begin{myCor}\label{cor:satisfied_path}
    If $B\in \mathbb{A}$, there exists a path (i.e., a leaf node to the root node) in which each sub access structure is satisfied by $B$. We call it a satisfied path.\qed
\end{myCor}


\subsection{Shamir Secret Sharing (SS)}

\begin{definition}[Shamir SS]\label{def:SSS}
Shamir secret sharing (SS) scheme allows a dealer to split a secret into $n$ shares for $n$ shareholders. With only a threshold $t\ (\leq n)$ shares, the secret can be successfully recovered, while fewer than $t$ shares reveal nothing about the secret. Shamir SS is defined with following two algorithms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\{s_1,s_2,\cdots,s_n\} \gets \shamirShare(s,n,t)$: Inputs a secret $s\in\ZZ_q$, $n$ and $t$, chooses a polynomial $f(x)=s+\sum_{i=1}^{t-1} a_i x^i$, where $a_i\xleftarrow[]{R} \ZZ_q$. Finally, the algorithm outputs $n$ shares $f(i)$, $i\in[1,n]$.
    \item $s\gets \shamirRecon(Q)$: Inputs any $t$ shares $Q\subseteq \{s_1,s_2,\cdots,s_n\}$, reconstructs the secret $s$ via the Lagrange interpolation.
\end{itemize}

\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Shamir SS on Group $\GG$]\label{def:GShamir}
Definition~\ref{def:SSS} has introduced the original Shamir secret sharing scheme, where a secret $s\in\ZZ_q$ is shared and recovered. Here we consider how to share secret $S\in\GG$ when the dealer does not know the value $s$ such that $S=g^s$. The Shamir secret sharing scheme on a group of order $q$ is defined as below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\{S_1,S_2,\cdots,S_n\} \gets \shamirGShare(S,n,t)$: Inputs a secret $S\in\GG$, $n$ and $t$, chooses a polynomial $f(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{t-1} a_i x^i$, where $a_i\xleftarrow[]{R} \ZZ_q$. Set $S_i=S\cdot g^{f(i)}$ for $i\in[1,n]$. The value $S_0=S$ is only the interpolation target and is never output as a share.
    \item $S\gets \shamirGRecon(Q)$: Inputs any $t$ shares $Q$, reconstructs the secret $S \gets S_0 = \prod_{i\in I}S_i^{\prod_{j\in I,j\neq i}\frac{-j}{i-j}}$, where $I$ is the set containing indexes of the shares $Q$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Shamir SS or Shamir SS on Group $\GG$ should satisfy: \textbf{Correctness}: If the secret sharing process is correctly followed, any authorized set of participants can reconstruct the secret. \textbf{Secrecy}: No information about the secret is revealed to any unauthorized set of participants.


\subsection{Linear Secret Sharing Scheme (LSSS)}
\begin{definition}[LSSS~\cite{Beimel2011LSSS,Beimel2025GSS,Lewko2011DABE}]
\label{def:LSSS}
Let $\mathcal{P}$ be a set of shareholders. A linear secret sharing scheme over a ring $\ZZ_q$ for an access structure $\mathbb{A}$ is a tuple $(M, \rho)$, where $M \in \ZZ_q^{|\mathbb{A}| \times l}$ is a share-generating matrix with $|\mathbb{A}|$ rows and $l$ columns
\footnote{We can consider \(\mathbb{A}\) as an access control tree, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:boolean_acp} and Figure~\ref{fig:threshold_acp}, where \(|\mathbb{A}|\) denotes the number of leaf nodes—or equivalently, the number of secret shares. The value of \(l\), however, depends on the specific parsing algorithm introduced in~\cite{Lewko2011DABE,Liu2010LSSS}.} and $\rho$ is a row-labeling function. The scheme consists of the following two algorithms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\{s_1,s_2,\cdots,s_{|\mathbb{A}|}\} \gets \lsssshare(s, \mathbb{A})$: To share a value $s \in \ZZ_q$ under access structure $\mathbb{A}$, randomly choose $v_2, \dots, v_{|\mathbb{A}|} \xleftarrow{R} \ZZ_q$ and set $\vec{v} = [s, v_2, \dots,$ $v_{|\mathbb{A}|}]^{\top}$. Then, $[s_1,\cdots,s_{|\mathbb{A}|}] \gets M\vec{v}$ is the vector of shares, where $s_i \in \ZZ_q$ belongs to shareholder $\rho(i)$ for each $i \in [1,\cdots, |\mathbb{A}|]$.
    \item $s \gets \lsssrecon(\mathbb{A}, Q)$: Let $Q=\{(i,s_i)\}_{i\in I_Q}$ be a labelled set of available shares whose labels satisfy $\mathbb{A}$, where $I_Q$ denotes the corresponding row indices of $M$. Let $M_Q \in \ZZ_q^{|I_Q| \times l}$ be the matrix formed by taking the subset of rows in $M$ that are indexed by $I_Q$. Only if $Q$ is an authorized set, there exists a vector $\vec{w}_Q \in \ZZ_q^{|I_Q|}$ such that $\vec{w}_Q^{\top} M_Q = [1, 0, \dots, 0]$. (Note that $\vec{w}_Q$ can be calculated using Gaussian elimination algorithm.)
    Further, the secret $s$ can be reconstructed by $\sum_{i \in I_Q} \vec{w}_i s_i$.
\end{itemize}

% \textbf{Remark:} Access control can be represented either as $\mathbb{A}$~\cite{Rouselakis2015EfficientSL} or $(M, \rho)$~\cite{Lewko2011DABE}. Denote $|\mathbb{A}|$ as the number of leaf nodes of an access structure $\mathbb{A}$ or number of rows of matrix $M$.
Similar to Shamir SS, LSSS should also satisfy \textbf{correctness} and \textbf{secrecy}.
\end{definition}





\subsection{Sigma Protocol and NIZK Proofs}
\label{sec:sigma_protocol}


Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be a relation, and define the corresponding language as \(L_\mathcal{R}=\{x\mid \exists w:(x,w)\in\mathcal{R}\}\). The values \(x\) and \(w\) are called the statement and the witness, respectively. A Sigma protocol \((P_1,P_2,V)\) is a three-move public-coin protocol~\cite{damgaard2002sigma}: the prover sends a first message \(a\), the verifier samples a challenge \(e\), and the prover answers with a response \(z\). The verifier accepts if \(V(x,a,e,z)=1\). Applying the Fiat-Shamir transform~\cite{fiat1986prove}, the challenge is computed as a hash of the public statement and the first message. 

\paragraph{\textbf{Schnorr-Type NIZK Proofs:}}\phantomsection\label{par:dex_proof_system_assumptions}
The basic Schnorr relation is
$
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{Sch}}
=\{((B,U),w): U=B^w\}.
$
The prover samples \(r\xleftarrow{R}\ZZ_q\), computes
$A=B^r, c=H(B,U,A),$
and returns \(\pi=(A,c,z)\), where \(z=r-cw\). The verifier recomputes \(c\) and accepts only if
$A = U^c B^z .$
Indeed, for an honestly generated proof,
$U^cB^z=(B^w)^cB^{r-cw}=B^r=A.$

\paragraph{\textbf{DLEQ Proofs:}}
The DLEQ proof is the Fiat-Shamir transformed Chaum-Pedersen protocol for the discrete-logarithm equality relation~\cite{Chaum1992DLEQ}
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{DLEQ}}
=\{((g,X,Y,Z),x): X=g^x \land Z=Y^x\}.$
A non-interactive DLEQ proof system consists of
$\pi\gets \dleq((g,X,Y,Z),x)$ and $0/1\gets \dleqVrf(g,X,Y,Z,\pi),$
where the $\dleq$ algorithm uses a witness \(x\in\ZZ_q\) satisfying \(X=g^x\) and \(Z=Y^x\).
Concretely, the algorithm samples \(r\xleftarrow{R}\ZZ_q\), computes
$T_1=g^r, T_2=Y^r, e=H(g,X,Y,Z,T_1,T_2),$
and returns \(\pi=(T_1,T_2,e,z)\), where \(z=r-ex\). The $\dleqVrf$ algorithm recomputes the challenge \(e\) and accepts only if
$T_1 = X^e g^z$ and $T_2 = Z^e Y^z .$
Completeness follows from
$X^eg^z=(g^x)^eg^{r-ex}=g^r=T_1$ and $Z^eY^z=(Y^x)^eY^{r-ex}=Y^r=T_2$ .

We rely on the standard properties of Fiat-Shamir transformed Sigma protocols in the random oracle model. \textbf{Completeness} states that honestly generated proofs for true statements are accepted. \textbf{Knowledge soundness} states that, except with negligible probability, any accepting proof implies the existence and efficient extractability of witnesses for the corresponding relation. \textbf{Zero-knowledge} states that proofs can be simulated without the witnesses and therefore reveal no information beyond the truth of the statements.






\section{Generalized Secret Sharing (GSS)}
\label{sec:gss}
We first define two types of GSS schemes, which differ based on the shared secret, $s\in \ZZ_q$ or $S\in \GG$.
% In this section, we first give a formal definition about GSS and introduce a construction based on LSSS. Further, we implement GSS on group $\GG$ based on Shamir SS and LSSS, respectively.
\subsection{Definition}
\begin{definition}[GSS]\label{def:GSS}
A generalized secret sharing (GSS) scheme~\cite{benaloh1990generalized} allows a dealer to share a secret $s\in\ZZ_q$ using arbitrary monotone access structure. The secret $s$ can be recovered only given an authorized set. A GSS scheme is defined by the following two algorithms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\{{s_1},{s_2},\cdots,{s_{|\mathbb{A}|}}\} \gets \gssshare(s, \mathbb{A})$: Inputs a value $s\in\ZZ_q$ and an access structure $\mathbb{A}$, outputs the labelled secret shares $\{{s_1},{s_2},\cdots,$ ${s_{|\mathbb{A}|}}\}$ in the canonical leaf order of $\mathbb{A}$, where $|\mathbb{A}|$ is the number of leaf nodes under access tree $\mathbb{A}$.
    % —or equivalently, the rows of the matrix $M$ after transforming $\mathbb{A}$ to LSSS matrix $M$ and row-labeling algorithm $\rho$.
    \item $s\gets \gssrecon(\mathbb{A}, Q)$: Inputs an access structure $\mathbb{A}$ and a labelled authorized share set $Q=\{(i,s_i)\}_{i\in I_Q}$, reconstructs the secret $s$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[GSS on Group $\GG$]\label{def:GGSS}
% Definition~\ref{def:GSS} has introduced the original Shamir secret sharing scheme, where a secret $s\in\ZZ_q$ is shared and recovered.
Here we consider how to share secret $S\in\GG$ and the dealer does not know the value $s$ such that $S=g^s$. The GSS on group $\GG$ of order $q$ is defined as below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\{{S_1},{S_2},\cdots,{S_{|\mathbb{A}|}}\} \gets \ggssshare (S, \mathbb{A})$: Inputs a value $S\in\GG$ and an access structure $\mathbb{A}$, outputs the labelled group shares $\{{S_1},{S_2},\cdots,$ ${S_{|\mathbb{A}|}}\}$ in the same canonical leaf order as Definition~\ref{def:GSS}.
    \item $S\gets \ggssrecon(\mathbb{A}, Q)$: Inputs an access structure $\mathbb{A}$ and a labelled authorized group-share set $Q=\{(i,S_i)\}_{i\in I_Q}$, reconstructs the secret $S$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

Similar to Shamir SS, GSS and GSS on Group $\GG$ should also satisfy \textbf{correctness} and \textbf{secrecy}.

\qed
\end{proof}

\end{proof}

\subsection{GSS Testing}
The reconstruction algorithm \(\gssrecon\) only needs an authorized subset of shares. This is sufficient for recovering the secret, but it is not sufficient for public verification in PVGSS: the verifier must check that the entire vector appearing in the proof response is one globally consistent sharing of a claimed response secret. Otherwise, a malicious dealer could make some authorized subsets reconstruct correctly while other leaves are inconsistent with the declared access structure. We capture this global consistency check as GSS testing.

\begin{definition}[GSS Testing]\label{def:gss_testing}
Let \(\mathbb{A}\) be an access structure with \(|\mathbb{A}|\) leaves in canonical order. A GSS testing algorithm
\[
    b\gets \gsstest(\mathbb{A},\vec{x},x)
\]
takes a candidate share vector \(\vec{x}=(x_1,\ldots,x_{|\mathbb{A}|})\in \ZZ_q^{|\mathbb{A}|}\) and a candidate secret \(x\in\ZZ_q\), and outputs \(b\in\{0,1\}\). It is complete if \(\gsstest(\mathbb{A},\vec{x},x)=1\) for every honestly generated vector \(\vec{x}\gets \gssshare(x,\mathbb{A})\). It is sound with error \(\epsilon\) if, except with probability at most \(\epsilon\), acceptance implies that \(\vec{x}\) is a valid output of \(\gssshare(x,\mathbb{A})\).
\end{definition}

We use the following two testing procedures for threshold-gate access trees, using the notation of Definitions~\ref{def:lagrange_interpolation} and~\ref{def:rs_dual_code}. These tests apply not only to the recursive Shamir realization of GSS, but also to the LSSS realization used in this work. Specifically, our LSSS matrix is obtained from a threshold-gate access tree by the conversion of Liu et al.~\cite{Liu2010LSSS}. In that conversion, expanding a \(t\)-of-\(m\) gate appends a local Vandermonde block over \(\ZZ_q\), so the values assigned to the children of each internal gate are still evaluations of a degree-\(<t\) polynomial whose constant term is the value assigned to the parent. Hence the resulting LSSS share vector preserves the same local Reed-Solomon structure as the tree representation, and GSS testing can be performed on the canonical leaf order induced by the tree.

For an internal node \(v\), let \(t_v\) be its threshold, let \(m_v\) be the number of its children, and let \(\alpha_{v,1},\ldots,\alpha_{v,m_v}\in\ZZ_q^*\) be their distinct evaluation points. Let \(r\) denote the root of \(\mathbb{A}\), and let \(Q=\{(i,x_i)\}_{i\in[|\mathbb{A}|]}\) denote the candidate leaf shares represented by \(\vec{x}\).

\textcircled{1} \emph{Exact interpolation test.}
Sample a minimal authorized subset \(Q^*\subseteq Q\). Equivalently, for each internal node \(v\), choose a set \(I_v\subseteq[m_v]\) of size \(t_v\) that satisfies \(v\), and recurse on the selected children until the selected leaves are exactly \(Q^*\). The test assigns the claimed value \(z_r:=x\) to the root. For every internal node \(v\), after the values \(\{z_{v,j}\}_{j\in I_v}\) of the selected children have been obtained recursively, define the unique polynomial
\[
    f_v(X)=\sum_{j\in I_v} z_{v,j}\ell_j^{I_v}(X)
    \quad\text{with}\quad \deg(f_v)<t_v .
\]
Here the basis polynomials \(\ell_j^{I_v}\) are defined with respect to the local evaluation points \(\{\alpha_{v,j}\}_{j\in I_v}\).
The local root consistency condition is \(f_v(0)=z_v\). For every unselected child \(j\notin I_v\), set \(z_{v,j}:=f_v(\alpha_{v,j})\) and verify the corresponding child subtree using \(z_{v,j}\) as its claimed value; for a leaf child, this verification is the equality check \(z_{v,j}=x_i\) against the corresponding entry of \(Q\). The test accepts if and only if all local root-consistency checks and all leaf comparisons accept. Acceptance therefore determines, for every internal node \(v\), a degree-\(<t_v\) polynomial whose evaluations agree with all candidate descendants. Hence the accepted vector is a valid recursive Shamir sharing of \(x\). The random choice of \(Q^*\) only fixes an interpolation basis and does not introduce soundness error.

\textcircled{2} \emph{Dual-code test.}
For each internal node \(v\), form the extended evaluation-point vector
\[
    \overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_v=(0,\alpha_{v,1},\ldots,\alpha_{v,m_v})
\]
and the corresponding local Reed-Solomon code
\[
    \mathcal{C}_v=\mathsf{RS}_{\overline{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_v}(m_v+1,t_v).
\]
A locally consistent assignment at \(v\) is precisely a vector
\((z_v,z_{v,1},\ldots,z_{v,m_v})\in\mathcal{C}_v\), where \(z_v\) is the value assigned to \(v\) and \(z_{v,j}\) is the value assigned to its \(j\)-th child. The verifier reconstructs the internal-node values from the leaves in a bottom-up manner and checks the root condition \(z_r=x\). Simultaneously, for each \(v\), it samples a random dual constraint \(\boldsymbol{h}_v\in\mathcal{C}_v^\perp\), or equivalently a random linear combination of a basis of \(\mathcal{C}_v^\perp\), and checks
\[
    \langle \boldsymbol{h}_v,(z_v,z_{v,1},\ldots,z_{v,m_v})\rangle=0.
\]
Equivalently, all local dual constraints may be aggregated into one random global parity check. If the candidate assignment is not a valid recursive Shamir sharing of \(x\), then at least one local syndrome is nonzero or the root condition fails. A random dual-code check misses a nonzero syndrome with probability at most \(1/q\)~\cite{Cascudo2017PVSS}; therefore the soundness error is bounded by \(1/q\).


\begin{lemma}[Soundness of GSS Testing]\label{lem:gss_testing_soundness}
% TODO添加引用
For recursive Shamir GSS over \(\ZZ_q\), and for the Liu et al. tree-to-LSSS realization used in this paper, the exact interpolation test is complete and sound with error \(0\). The dual-code test is complete and sound with error at most \(1/q\).
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Completeness of both procedures follows from the local correctness of Shamir sharing at every threshold gate. In an honestly generated recursive sharing, the values assigned to the children of each internal \(t\)-of-\(m\) gate are evaluations of a polynomial of degree less than \(t\), whose constant term is the value assigned to that gate. Therefore, Lagrange interpolation reconstructs the correct local polynomial, all recomputed child values agree with the candidate vector, all dual-code parity checks vanish, and the root value is the shared secret \(x\).

For soundness of the exact interpolation test, suppose the test accepts. The interpolation phase fixes, for every internal gate, a polynomial of degree less than the gate threshold. The subsequent comparison phase verifies that every unselected child value agrees with the corresponding evaluation of this polynomial, while the selected child values agree by construction. By induction from the leaves to the root, every subtree is therefore a valid recursive Shamir sharing of the value reconstructed at its root. Since the final root check requires this value to equal \(x\), the full vector \(\vec{x}\) is a valid sharing of \(x\). Thus the exact test has soundness error \(0\).

For the dual-code test, any invalid candidate vector violates at least one local Reed-Solomon consistency condition or reconstructs a root value different from \(x\). The violated local consistency condition corresponds to a nonzero syndrome with respect to the relevant dual Reed-Solomon code. A random linear combination of the induced dual constraints evaluates to zero on this nonzero syndrome with probability at most \(1/q\) over the verifier's random choice. Thus an invalid vector is accepted with probability at most \(1/q\).
\qed
\end{proof}

\section{Publicly Verifiable Generalized Secret Sharing}
\label{sec:pvgss}
\subsection{Definition}
\begin{definition}[PVGSS]\label{def:PVGSS}
 A publicly verifiable generalized secret sharing (PVGSS) scheme allows a dealer to split a secret using an arbitrary monotone access structure in a publicly verifiable manner. Shares are encrypted and can be delivered over a public channel. The correctness of encrypted shares and decrypted reconstruction shares can be verified by any external party. The ordered public-key list is always aligned with the canonical leaf order of the access structure. The PVGSS scheme includes the following algorithms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(\sk_i,\pk_i) \gets \pvgssSetup(\lambda)$: Inputs security parameter $\lambda$, outputs a key pair ($\sk_i, \pk_i$) for shareholder $P_i$.
    \item $(\{C_i\}, \proofs_s)\gets \pvgssShare(s,\mathbb{A}, \{\pk_i\})$: Inputs a secret $s\in\ZZ_q$, an access structure $\mathbb{A}$, and an ordered list of public keys $\{\pk_i\}_{i\in[|\mathbb{A}|]}$, outputs encrypted shares $\{C_i\}$ and the corresponding NIZK proof $\proofs_s$.
    \item $(0/1) \gets\pvgssVerify(\{C_i\}, \proofs_s,\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\})$: Inputs encrypted shares $\{C_i\}$, proof $\proofs_s$, access structure $\mathbb{A}$, and ordered public keys, outputs 1 if the shares are correctly encrypted. Otherwise, outputs 0.
    \item $(S_i, \proofs_{S_i}) \gets \pvgssPreRecon(C_i, \sk_i)$: Inputs ciphertext $C_i$ and the corresponding secret key $\sk_i$, outputs a decrypted share $S_i$ and the attached NIZK proof $\proofs_{S_i}$.
    \item $(0/1) \gets \pvgssKeyVrf(C_i, S_i, \proofs_{S_i}, \pk_i)$: Inputs the encrypted share $C_i$, the decrypted share $S_i$, the NIZK proof $\proofs_{S_i}$, and public key $\pk_i$, outputs 1 if $S_i$ is a correct decryption of $C_i$.
    \item $(S) \gets \pvgssRecon(\mathbb{A},Q)$: Inputs the access structure $\mathbb{A}$ and a labelled authorized set of decrypted shares $Q=\{(i,S_i)\}_{i\in I_Q}$, outputs the recovered secret $S(=g^s)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

PVGSS scheme is required to achieve below security properties, which are formally defined in Appendix~\ref{sec:pvgss_properties}.

$\bullet$ \textbf{Correctness}: The scheme ensures that if the secret sharing process is correctly followed, any authorized subset of participants can reconstruct the secret $S$.

$\bullet$ \textbf{Verifiability}: Anyone can verify that the encrypted shares provided by the dealer are correct without knowing the actual secret. Besides, users can also check correctness of the decrypted shares in the reconstruction phase.

$\bullet$ \textbf{IND1-secrecy}: The secret remains hidden from unauthorized parties prior to the reconstruction phase.
% This property is formally defined by Definition~\ref{def:ind1secrecy}.



\subsection{PVGSS Construction Based on GSS}
\label{sec:pvgss_based_on_gss}
Usually, PVSS schemes are constructed based on SS. Informally speaking, PVSS combines SS and public key encryption with verifiability. Intuitively, PVGSS can be constructed similarly by replacing SS with GSS.
We leverage the original GSS scheme in the sharing phase and employ GSS on group $\GG$ in the reconstruction phase.
Figure~\ref{fig:pvgss_from_ss} illustrate our mindset of constructions vividly.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvgss_using_gss} demonstrates the general PVGSS construction by taking advantage of GSS scheme and NIZK proofs. The sharing proofs are Schnorr-type NIZK proofs obtained through the Fiat-Shamir transform~\cite{fiat1986prove}.
The initiation phase comprises the \(\pvgssSetup\) algorithm. The sharing phase involves the \(\pvgssShare\) and \(\pvgssVerify\) algorithms. The reconstruction phase includes \(\pvgssPreRecon\), \(\pvgssKeyVrf\), and \(\pvgssRecon\) algorithms.


In the $\pvgssSetup$ algorithm, each shareholder $P_i$ generates its key pair $(\sk_i,\pk_i)$, where $\pk_i=g^{\sk_i}$ and $\sk_i$ is randomly chosen from $\ZZ_q^*$.

In the $\pvgssShare$ algorithm, the dealer first invokes $\gssshare$ algorithm for secret $s\in\ZZ_q$ to generate secret shares based on the access structure $\mathbb{A}$. Note that a PVGSS is not merely a combination of multiple PVSS instances, since it outputs encrypted shares only for the leaf nodes. As previously introduced, \(|\mathbb{A}|\) denotes the number of leaf nodes in the access structure \(\mathbb{A}\). Then, it hides the $|\mathbb{A}|$ shares directly using the targeted shareholder's public key in the same leaf order, i.e., $\pk_i^{s_i}$. Next, it generates Schnorr-type Fiat-Shamir NIZK proofs. Specifically, $\{C'_i\}$, $c$, and $\{\hat{s}_i\}$ are the commitment, the challenge value, and the response values, respectively. The challenge binds the ordered public keys and the access structure. Therefore, the NIZK proof $\proofs_s$ is $(\{C'_i\}, c, \hat{s}, \{\hat{s}_i\})$.
To this end, the dealer has successfully generated encrypted secret shares $\{C_i\}$ and the corresponding NIZK proof $\proofs_s$ which can be adopted to prove the dealer's honesty.
Different from the LDEI protocol in Albatross~\cite{cascudo2020albatross}, which directly outputs the polynomial $\hat{p}(X)$ as part of the NIZK response values, PVGSS transmits $|\mathbb{A}|$ polynomial evaluation points $\{\hat{s}_i\}$ of $\hat{p}(X)$.

        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}$\hat{s}_i \gets s'_i - c\cdot s_i \ \forall i\in [1,\ldots,|\mathbb{A}|]$\\
        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}$\proofs_s \gets (\{C'_i\}, c, \hat{s},\{\hat{s}_i\})$\\

        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}$c\overset{?}{=} H(\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\},\{C_i\}, \{C'_i\})$\\
        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em} $C'_i \overset{?}{=} C_i^c\cdot \pk_i ^{\hat{s}_i} \ \forall i\in [1,\ldots,|\mathbb{A}|]$\\
        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em} $1\overset{?}{=} \gsstest(\mathbb{A},\{\hat{s}_i\}_{i\in[1,\ldots,|\mathbb{A}|]},\hat{s})$\\
        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}return 1 iff all checks pass\\
    \underline{$(S_i, \proofs_{S_i}) \gets \pvgssPreRecon(C_i, \sk_i):$} \\

        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}$S_i=C_i^{1/\sk_i}=g^{s_i}$\\

    \underline{$(0/1) \gets \pvgssKeyVrf(C_i, S_i, \proofs_{S_i}, \pk_i):$} \\
        % \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}$e(S_i,\pk_i)\overset{?}{=}e(C_i, g)$ \\
        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}reject if $\pk_i$, $C_i$, or $S_i$ is invalid\\

        \setlength{\parindent}{1.8em}$S\gets \ggssrecon(\mathbb{A},Q)$ where $S=g^s$ and $Q=\{(i,S_i)\}_{i\in I_Q}$ is a labelled authorized set of $\mathbb{A}$.

In the $\pvgssVerify$ algorithm, any (external) party can check whether the dealer has honestly performed the sharing phase.
Due to the presence of \(|\mathbb{A}|\) independent shares \(\{s_i\}\), the verifier checks group membership, recomputes the Fiat-Shamir challenge, and then verifies two algebraic conditions.
The first equation (executed \( |\mathbb{A}| \) times) is a Schnorr-type proof of knowledge for each encrypted share \(s_i\).
The second check invokes \(\gsstest\) directly to verify that all response values $\{\hat{s}_i\}$ are valid GSS shares of the response secret \(\hat{s}\). It binds the shares embedded in \(\{C_i\}\) to the secret \(s\) under the access structure \(\mathbb{A}\).
Refer to Theorem~\ref{theo:correctness} for details about the correctness.

In the $\pvgssPreRecon$ algorithm, the shareholder, holding $\sk_i$, decrypts $C_i$ with its secret key $\sk_i$ and obtains $S_i=g^{s_i}$.
Additionally, the shareholder employs the discrete logarithm equality (DLEQ) algorithm~\cite{Chaum1992DLEQ} to generate a non-interactive zero-knowledge (NIZK) proof \(\proofs_{S_i}\), demonstrating that \(\log_g \pk_i = \log_{S_i} C_i = \sk_i\), i.e., \(C_i=S_i^{\sk_i}\).
% Note that original secret share $s_i$ is still kept unknown.

The \(\pvgssKeyVrf\) algorithm directly invokes the corresponding DLEQ verification algorithm, \(\dleqVrf\), to verify the correctness of \(S_i\) provided by the shareholder. Note that no information about $\sk_i$ is leaked during this process.


Given enough authorized decrypted shares, i.e., an authorized set $Q$, the $\pvgssRecon$ algorithm can reconstruct a secret $S$ using $\ggssrecon$ algorithm. Note that \(\ggssrecon\), rather than \(\gssrecon\), is used to recover the secret, since in our PVGSS scheme the secret \(S\) resides in \(\GG\), consistent with many prior PVSS constructions~\cite{Cascudo2017PVSS,cascudo2020albatross}.




\subsection{Complexity Analysis}
\label{sec:pvgss_complexity}

% Recall that:
% 1) PVGSS schemes output encrypted shares only for the leaf nodes defined by the access structure \(\mathbb{A}\);
% 2) \(|\mathbb{A}|\) denotes the number of leaf nodes in \(\mathbb{A}\); and
% 3) \(Q\) represents an authorized set under \(\mathbb{A}\).

\textbf{Computational Complexity:}
We first count the dominant group operations in \(\GG\), and then account for the public finite-field computations that affect the concrete running time. Matrix operations in the LSSS-based construction, polynomial evaluation and Lagrange interpolation in the Shamir SS-based construction, Exact/Dual GSS testing, and LSSS reconstruction-weight computation do not change the group-operation asymptotics. However, they are not negligible in the implementation, and they explain part of the curve separation observed in the experiments.

In the sharing phase, the $\pvgssShare$ algorithm costs $2|\mathbb{A}|$ group exponentiations, where each $C_i$ and $C'_i$ requires an exponentiation. The $\pvgssVerify$ algorithm requires $2|\mathbb{A}|$ exponentiations to verify the NIZK equation for each encrypted share, plus one public GSS testing procedure over the responses. The Exact interpolation test and the Dual-code test are both public finite-field checks over the access structure; they preserve linear group-operation complexity but lead to different concrete verification costs. Therefore, the distribution complexity is \(O(|\mathbb{A}|)\) in group operations, with implementation-level constants affected by the chosen GSS testing routine.
In the reconstruction phase, the $\pvgssPreRecon$ algorithm costs a constant number of exponentiations for each shareholder, including the calculation of $\proofs_{S_i}$ from DLEQ. The $\pvgssKeyVrf$ algorithm requires 4 exponentiations as required by \(\dleqVrf\). The online $\pvgssRecon$ algorithm takes $|Q|$ exponentiations, where $Q$ denotes the authorized set for the access structure $\mathbb{A}$. For LSSS-based reconstruction, the public reconstruction weights may be computed from the access matrix and authorized set; for fixed DEX access structures and authorized sets these weights can be precomputed, so the online reconstruction remains a group linear combination. For Shamir SS-based reconstruction, Lagrange interpolation on the selected path contributes to the measured running time. Thus, the online reconstruction complexity is $O(|Q|)$ in group operations, while the concrete cost depends on whether these public coefficients are prepared in advance.



\textbf{Communicational Complexity:} In the PVGSS sharing phase, the dealer outputs ($\{C_i\}, \proofs_s$), which consists of $2|\mathbb{A}|$ $\GG$ elements and ($|\mathbb{A}|+2$) $\ZZ_q$ elements.
During the reconstruction phase, each user needs to collect $(3\GG+2\ZZ_q)|Q|$ elements, as each correct $\proofs_{S_i}$ contains 2 elements on $\GG$ and 2 elements on $\ZZ_q$.
Therefore, the communication complexity is \(O(|\mathbb{A}|)\) during the sharing phase and \(O(|Q|)\) during the reconstruction phase, respectively.

\begin{theorem}[Correctness]\label{theo:correctness}
The PVGSS construction satisfies correctness defined by Definition~\ref{def:Correctness}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
  Fix an arbitrary security parameter \(\lambda\), access structure
  \(\mathbb{A}\), secret \(s\in\ZZ_q\), and ordered valid public-key
  list
  \(\{\pk_i\}_{i\in[|\mathbb{A}|]}\), where \(\pk_i=g^{\sk_i}\) and
  \(\sk_i\in\ZZ_q^*\). Let
  \[
      (\{C_i\},\proofs_s)\gets
      \pvgssShare(s,\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\})
  \]
  be an honest sharing. By the definition of \(\pvgssShare\), the
  dealer first
  samples a valid GSS sharing
  \[
      (s_1,\ldots,s_{|\mathbb{A}|})\gets \gssshare(s,\mathbb{A}), \qquad C_i=\pk_i^{s_i},
  \]
  and publishes \(C_i=\pk_i^{s_i}\) for every leaf \(i\). It also
  samples
  \(s'\in\ZZ_q\), computes
  \[
      (s'_1,\ldots,s'_{|\mathbb{A}|})\gets \gssshare(s',\mathbb{A}),
      \qquad C'_i=\pk_i^{s'_i},
  \]
  sets
  \[
      c=H(\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\},\{C_i\},\{C'_i\}),\qquad
      \hat{s}=s'-cs,\qquad
      \hat{s}_i=s'_i-cs_i .
  \]
  We first show that \(\pvgssVerify\) accepts. Since all values are
  generated
  honestly, the verifier recomputes the same Fiat-Shamir challenge
  \(c\). Moreover,
  for every \(i\),
  \[
      C_i^c\pk_i^{\hat{s}_i}
      =(\pk_i^{s_i})^c\pk_i^{s'_i-cs_i}
      =\pk_i^{s'_i}
      =C'_i .
  \]
  Thus all Schnorr-type verification equations hold. It remains to
  check the
  GSS-consistency equation. Because the underlying GSS sharing
  algorithm is linear,
  \[
      (\hat{s}_1,\ldots,\hat{s}_{|\mathbb{A}|})
      =(s'_1,\ldots,s'_{|\mathbb{A}|})
        -c(s_1,\ldots,s_{|\mathbb{A}|})
  \]
  is a valid GSS sharing of \(\hat{s}=s'-cs\) under the same access
  structure
  \(\mathbb{A}\). By completeness of \(\gsstest\),
  \[
      \gsstest(\mathbb{A},\{\hat{s}_i\}_{i\in[|\mathbb{A}|]},
  \hat{s})=1 .
  \]
  Therefore,
  \[
      \pvgssVerify(\{C_i\},\proofs_s,\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\})=1 .
  \]

  Next, consider any labelled authorized set
  \(Q=\{(i,S_i)\}_{i\in I_Q}\) of \(\mathbb{A}\). For every \(i\in
  I_Q\), honest
  execution of \(\pvgssPreRecon\) gives
  \[
      S_i=C_i^{1/\sk_i}
         =(\pk_i^{s_i})^{1/\sk_i}
         =(g^{\sk_i s_i})^{1/\sk_i}
         =g^{s_i}.
  \]
  The proof \(\proofs_{S_i}\) generated by \(\pvgssPreRecon\) is an
  honest DLEQ
  proof for the statement
  \[
      \log_g \pk_i=\log_{S_i} C_i=\sk_i ,
  \]
  since \(C_i=S_i^{\sk_i}\). By completeness of the DLEQ proof
  system,
  \[
      \pvgssKeyVrf(C_i,S_i,\proofs_{S_i},\pk_i)=1
      \quad\text{for all } i\in I_Q .
  \]

  Finally, since \(Q\) is authorized and the values supplied to
  reconstruction are
  exactly the group shares \(S_i=g^{s_i}\) corresponding to a valid
  GSS sharing of
  \(s\), correctness of the underlying GSS-on-group reconstruction
  algorithm gives
  \[
      S' \gets \pvgssRecon(\mathbb{A},Q)
         = \ggssrecon(\mathbb{A},Q)
         = g^s .
  \]
  This correctness of \(\ggssrecon\) follows from the corresponding
  group-GSS
  instantiations proved in Sections~\ref{sec:gss_basedon_sss}
  and~\ref{sec:gss_basedon_lsss}.

  Combining the three conclusions, for every honestly generated
  execution and
  every authorized labelled set \(Q\), all verification algorithms
  accept and the
  reconstructed value equals \(g^s\). Hence the PVGSS construction satisfies correctness by Definition~\ref{def:Correctness}.
\qed
  \end{proof}



\begin{theorem}[Verifiability of Encrypted Shares]\label{theo:ver_enc_shares}
    If the Schnorr-type Fiat-Shamir proof used in \(\pvgssShare\) is knowledge-sound in the random oracle model and the instantiated GSS testing procedure is sound with error \(\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}\), then PVGSS satisfies verifiability of encrypted shares, as defined by Definition~\ref{def:Ver_enc_share}, up to the corresponding testing error. In particular, for recursive Shamir GSS, Lemma~\ref{lem:gss_testing_soundness} gives \(\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}=0\) for the exact interpolation test and \(\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}\leq 1/q\) for the dual-code test.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathsf{venc}}_{\adv}\) denote the experiment in Definition~\ref{def:Ver_enc_share}. The experiment outputs \(1\) only if \(\adv\) produces \((\{C_i\},\proofs_s)\) such that \(\pvgssVerify\) accepts, but there is no secret \(s\) and no valid GSS sharing \(\{s_i\}\) of \(s\) satisfying \(C_i=\pk_i^{s_i}\) for all \(i\).

Consider an accepting proof
$\proofs_s=(\{C'_i\},c,\hat{s},\{\hat{s}_i\})$,
for the statement \((\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\},\{C_i\})\). By knowledge soundness of the Fiat-Shamir Schnorr proof in the ROM, except with probability
\[
    \mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{ks}}_{\mathsf{FS}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{FS}})
    +\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
\]
for a PPT extractor adversary \(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{FS}}\), the extractor obtains a second accepting transcript with the same Sigma-protocol commitment and a distinct challenge:
\[
    (\{C'_i\},\tilde c,\tilde{s},\{\tilde{s}_i\})
    \qquad\text{where } c\neq \tilde c .
\]
Both transcripts pass the algebraic verification equation, and hence, for every leaf \(i\),
\[
    C'_i=C_i^c\pk_i^{\hat{s}_i}
        =C_i^{\tilde c}\pk_i^{\tilde{s}_i}.
\]
Since all public keys are validated as non-identity elements of the prime-order group and \(c-\tilde c\neq 0\), define
\[
    s_i=\frac{\tilde{s}_i-\hat{s}_i}{c-\tilde c}
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    s=\frac{\tilde{s}-\hat{s}}{c-\tilde c}.
\]
The preceding equation immediately gives \(C_i=\pk_i^{s_i}\) for every \(i\).

It remains to show that the extracted exponents form a valid GSS sharing of \(s\). Since both transcripts are accepting, they also satisfy
\[
    \gsstest(\mathbb{A},\{\hat{s}_i\},\hat{s})=1
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \gsstest(\mathbb{A},\{\tilde{s}_i\},\tilde{s})=1.
\]
By soundness of GSS testing, except with probability at most \(2\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}\), the vectors \(\{\hat{s}_i\}\) and \(\{\tilde{s}_i\}\) are valid GSS sharings of \(\hat{s}\) and \(\tilde{s}\), respectively. The underlying GSS sharing relation is linear, so the coordinate-wise linear combination
\[
    \left\{\frac{\tilde{s}_i-\hat{s}_i}{c-\tilde c}\right\}_{i\in[|\mathbb{A}|]}
\]
is a valid GSS sharing of \((\tilde{s}-\hat{s})/(c-\tilde c)=s\). Therefore the accepted ciphertext vector encrypts valid GSS shares of the secret \(s\), which contradicts the winning condition in Definition~\ref{def:Ver_enc_share}.

Consequently, any successful adversary must either break knowledge soundness of the Fiat-Shamir proof or cause an invalid vector to pass one of the two GSS tests. Thus
\[
    \Pr[\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathsf{venc}}_{\adv}=1]
    \leq
    \mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{ks}}_{\mathsf{FS}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{FS}})
    +2\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}
    +\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).
\]
For recursive Shamir GSS, Lemma~\ref{lem:gss_testing_soundness} gives \(\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}=0\) for the exact interpolation test and \(\epsilon_{\mathsf{test}}\leq 1/q\) for the dual-code test, yielding the claimed bound.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Verifiability of Share Decryption]\label{theo:ver_share_dec}
    If the DLEQ proof system used in \(\pvgssPreRecon\) satisfies the standard soundness property, then PVGSS satisfies verifiability of share decryption as defined by Definition~\ref{def:Ver_dec_share}.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathsf{vdec}}_{\adv}$ be the experiment in Definition~\ref{def:Ver_dec_share}; the experiment outputs $1$ when $\adv$ produces \((\pk,C,S,\proofs_S)\) such that \(\pvgssKeyVrf(C,S,\proofs_S,\pk)=1\), but there is no \(x\in\ZZ_q^*\) satisfying \(\pk=g^x\) and \(C=S^x\).

Since \(\pvgssKeyVrf\) first validates \(\pk,C,S\) and then invokes \(\dleqVrf(g,\pk,S,C,\proofs_S)\), an accepting adversarial output is an accepting DLEQ proof for the statement \((g,\pk,S,C)\). By DLEQ soundness, except with negligible probability, there exists \(x\in\ZZ_q\) such that \(\pk=g^x\) and \(C=S^x\). Registered public keys are validated as non-identity group elements, so this witness is nonzero and hence lies in \(\ZZ_q^*\). Therefore the winning condition of \(\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathsf{vdec}}_{\adv}\) can occur only with negligible probability:
\[
\Pr[\mathsf{Exp}^{\mathsf{vdec}}_{\adv}=1]
\leq
\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).
\]
In particular, when \(C=\pk_i^{s_i}\) is a PVGSS ciphertext for registered \(\pk_i=g^{\sk_i}\), the accepted DLEQ relation gives \(C=S^{\sk_i}\), hence \(S=C^{1/\sk_i}=g^{s_i}\). Therefore an invalid decrypted share is rejected except with negligible DLEQ soundness error.
\qed
\end{proof}


\begin{theorem}[IND1-secrecy]\label{theo:ind1secrecy}
The PVGSS constructions satisfy IND1-secrecy, defined by game Definition~\ref{def:ind1secrecy}, assuming (i) zero-knowledge of the Fiat-Shamir proofs in the random oracle model, (ii) DDH in \(\GG\), and (iii) privacy of the underlying GSS-on-group scheme against unauthorized labelled sets.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let \(\adv\) be a PPT adversary in the IND1 game. Let \(m=|\mathbb{A}^*|\), and let \(U\subseteq[m]\) be the unauthorized set chosen by \(\adv\). We write \(E_j\) for the event that \(\adv\) outputs \(b'=b\) in Game \(G_j\).

\textbf{Game \(G_0\).} This is the real IND1 experiment of Definition~\ref{def:ind1secrecy}. The challenger samples \(b\), runs
\[
(\{C_i^*\},\proofs_{x_b})\gets \pvgssShare(x_b,\mathbb{A}^*,\{\pk_i\}),
\]
and answers every allowed decryption query \(i\in U\) with
\((S_i^*,\proofs_{S_i}^*)\gets\pvgssPreRecon(C_i^*,\sk_i)\).
Thus \(\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{ind1}}_{\mathsf{PVGSS}}(\adv)=|\Pr[E_0]-1/2|\).

\textbf{Game \(G_1\): simulate sharing proofs.} This game is identical to \(G_0\), except that the NIZK proof \(\proofs_{x_b}\) generated in the sharing phase is replaced by a simulated proof for the same public statement. Since the proof is zero-knowledge in the random oracle model,
\[
\left|\Pr[E_0]-\Pr[E_1]\right|
\leq
\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{zk}}_{\mathsf{FS}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{zk}})
+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).
\]
The DLEQ proofs returned for allowed decryption queries are still honestly generated, since these queries correspond to real decrypted group shares.

\textbf{Game \(G_2\): randomize unopened ciphertexts.} Starting from \(G_1\), replace each unopened ciphertext \(C_i^*=\pk_i^{s_i}\) for \(i\notin U\) by an independently uniform group element \(R_i\in\GG\), one index at a time. The replacement keeps the public keys, the access structure, the simulated sharing proof, and all opened group shares for \(i\in U\) unchanged.

For each replaced index, a distinguisher between two adjacent games gives a DDH distinguisher: the reduction embeds the DDH tuple into \((g,\pk_i,g^{s_i},C_i^*)\), where \(g^{s_i}\) is the corresponding group share in the GSS-on-group sharing. If the tuple is Diffie-Hellman, the ciphertext has the real distribution \(\pk_i^{s_i}\); if the tuple is random, the ciphertext is uniform in \(\GG\). Therefore, by a standard hybrid argument over the \(m-|U|\) unopened indices,
\[
\left|\Pr[E_1]-\Pr[E_2]\right|
\leq
(m-|U|)\cdot \mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{ddh}}_{\GG}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{ddh}})
+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).
\]

\textbf{Game \(G_3\): replace unauthorized opened shares.} In \(G_2\), all ciphertexts for \(i\notin U\) are uniform and independent of \(b\). The only remaining values that may depend on \(b\) are the decrypted group shares \(\{S_i^*=g^{s_i}\}_{i\in U}\) returned to \(\adv\). Since \(U\) is unauthorized for \(\mathbb{A}^*\), privacy of the underlying GSS-on-group scheme implies that these group shares can be replaced by a simulated unauthorized view independent of whether the shared secret is \(g^{x_0}\) or \(g^{x_1}\). Thus
\[
\left|\Pr[E_2]-\Pr[E_3]\right|
\leq
\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{priv}}_{\mathsf{GrpGSS}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{gss}})
+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda).
\]

In \(G_3\), the adversary's entire view consists of simulated sharing proofs, uniform unopened ciphertexts, and simulated unauthorized opened group shares, all independent of \(b\). Hence \(\Pr[E_3]=1/2\). Combining the above bounds gives
\[
\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{ind1}}_{\mathsf{PVGSS}}(\adv)
\leq
\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{zk}}_{\mathsf{FS}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{zk}})
+(m-|U|)\cdot \mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{ddh}}_{\GG}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{ddh}})
+\mathsf{Adv}^{\mathsf{priv}}_{\mathsf{GrpGSS}}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{gss}})
+\mathsf{negl}(\lambda),
\]
which is negligible under the stated assumptions.
\qed
\end{proof}



\section{DEX Based on PVGSS}
\label{sec:dex_on_pvgss}






\subsection{High-Level Overview}



Based on the proposed PVGSS scheme, we design a DEX to allow exchangers to swap tokens fairly and simultaneously. In this research, we merely focus on ERC-20\footnote{\url{https://eips.ethereum.org/EIPS/eip-20}} token exchange. The DEX involves two roles: exchangers and watchers. Anyone can be exchangers and watchers. Each exchanger holds specific types of ERC-20 tokens, while multiple watchers collectively form a passive notary committee to address potential disputes.
Take two exchangers, i.e., Alice and Bob, and $n$ watchers ($W_1,\cdots, W_n$) as an example,  the access structure is defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dex_as}
  \mathbb{A} = (2\ of\ (Alice, Bob, (t\ of\ (W_1, W_2,\cdots, W_n))))
\end{equation}
% Figure~\ref{fig:swap_as} illustrates the concrete access structure incorporating threshold gates.
Based on Corollary~\ref{cor:as_proposition}, we can infer that the set [$Alice, Bob$] is an authorized set. Apparently, if both Alice and Bob are honest, they can recover any secrets of PVGSS instance.
If either exchanger is malicious, say Bob, Alice can recover the secret with help of at least $t$ watchers. 
% This shows the feature of fault tolerance, i.e., the access structure allows fault of one exchanger and $n-t$ watchers. 
Due to the designed incentive mechanism, fairness does not rely on assuming that more than \(t\) watchers are honest. In particular, \(t\) can be set to \(1\): one responsive and economically accountable watcher is sufficient to resolve a dispute, while non-responsive or faulty watchers are penalized.

The DEX operates under a commit‑and‑reveal paradigm.
As shown by Figure~\ref{fig:dex_overview}, the DEX optimistically runs in two communication rounds for Alice and Bob.
In the first round, $\mathsf{swap}_1$, each exchanger commits to a secret using $\pvgssShare$, where all the $n+2$ entities are considered as shareholders.
The correctness of the commitment is guaranteed by the $\pvgssVerify$ algorithm.
In the second round, $\mathsf{swap}_2$, each exchanger reveals its decrypted share using $\pvgssPreRecon$.
The correctness of share decryption is ensured by $\pvgssKeyVrf$. Then, both Alice and Bob jointly recover each other's secrets using $\pvgssRecon$.
In a pessimistic occasion where a player complains to the passive watchers, who will be involved to resolve dispute using $\pvgssPreRecon$.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Register:} Upon receiving $(\mathsf{register}, \swapid, \mathsf{pid}, \pk_y)$ from a party $y$, stores $\pk_y$ in its local state via $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}$ and sends the public event to $\adv$.
    \item \textbf{Deposit:} Upon receiving $(\mathsf{freeze}, \swapid, x, \mathsf{Token}_x, v_x)$ from exchanger $x$, instructs $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}$ to lock the tokens in escrow. Withdrawals are permitted only if the exchange either does not commence or terminates in $\mathsf{failure}$.
    \item \textbf{Commit (\(\mathsf{swap}_1\)):} Upon receiving $(\mathsf{commit}, \swapid, x)$ from exchanger $x$, records that $x$ has committed and sends the public event to $\adv$. The timer starts when both exchangers have committed.
    \item \textbf{Reveal (\(\mathsf{swap}_2\)):} Upon receiving $(\mathsf{reveal}, \swapid, x)$ from exchanger $x$ before $\Delta t_1$, records that $x$ has opened its commitment and sends the public event to $\adv$.

    \item \textbf{Determine:} Upon an explicit determine request:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If both exchangers have opened, instructs $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}$ to transfer tokens and output $(\mathsf{success})$.
        \item If exactly one exchanger has not opened but at least $t$ assigned watchers have opened for that exchanger, does the same as above.
        \item Otherwise, after $\Delta t_2$, instructs $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}$ to refund and output $(\mathsf{failure})$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Ideal functionality.}
Abstracting away from implementation details, any reasonable decentralized exchange must satisfy three semantic requirements: (i) atomic execution of asset transfers, ensuring that either all swaps complete or none do; (ii) absence of unilateral advantage, meaning that no party can obtain the counterparty's asset without releasing its own; and (iii) public verifiability of outcomes, allowing any observer to determine whether a swap has succeeded or failed.
We therefore define the ideal functionality $\dex$ of a decentralized exchange by Figure~\ref{fig:ideal_dex}.
The functionality $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}$ is defined in the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}$-hybrid model and relies on the ledger time and public state to enforce deadlines and asset transfers.
Time is modeled via global deadlines $\Delta t_1 < \Delta t_2$, enforced by $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}$.
If the functionality outputs \(\mathsf{success}\), the exchange completes fairly; otherwise, the exchange is aborted.
For notation, \(x\in\{A,B\}\) denotes an exchanger, \(\bar{x}\) denotes the counterparty, and \(K_y^{(x)}\) denotes party \(y\)'s decrypted PVGSS share for exchanger \(x\)'s commitment.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We introduced publicly verifiable generalized secret sharing (PVGSS), which combines expressive monotone access structures with non-interactive public accountability. The construction encrypts only the leaf shares of an underlying GSS instance and uses Schnorr-type Fiat-Shamir proofs together with GSS testing to certify that all encrypted shares are globally consistent with a single dealer secret. We instantiated the framework from recursive Shamir secret sharing and from LSSS, and proved correctness, verifiability of encrypted shares, verifiability of share decryption, and IND1-secrecy under explicit assumptions on DDH, the proof systems, and the privacy of the underlying GSS-on-group sharing.
We also demonstrated how PVGSS can serve as a cryptographic building block for accountable blockchain protocols through a decentralized exchange. In this application, PVGSS enables verifiable commitment, verifiable secret release, and public arbitration under a flexible access structure. 
% The resulting DEX achieves optimistic fairness and accountable arbitration under ledger-liveness and incentive-compatibility assumptions; in particular, fairness does not require an honest-watcher threshold, since the incentive mechanism allows the protocol to set \(t=1\). Our implementation and experiments show that the off-chain PVGSS algorithms remain practical for large access structures and that the on-chain DEX operations are feasible on Ethereum-compatible platforms.
Several directions remain open. 
First, PVGSS can be applied to other systems that require both expressive authorization and public auditability, including randomness beacons, bridges, channels, or oracle networks.
Second, it is useful to explore additional PVGSS constructions with smaller proofs, lower verification cost, or post-quantum assumptions. 
% Another important job is to extend GSS testing beyond tree-derived LSSS instances and support arbitrary LSSS matrices with efficient public consistency checks.
Finally, dynamic variants such as proactive PVGS, q-PVGSS and aggregatable PVGSS, analogous to proactive SS~\cite{Bagherpour2022VPSS}, q-PVSS~\cite{cascudo2024publicly} and aggregatable PVSS~\cite{Bacho2023Beacon}, may further broaden the applicability of publicly verifiable sharing under general access structures.

\section{Motivation of the Proposed DEX}

Centralized exchanges (CEXs), such as Binance\footnote{\url{https://www.binance.com/en}} and Coinbase\footnote{\url{https://www.coinbase.com}}, provide liquidity and usability but rely on central authorities, exposing users to breaches, mismanagement, or censorship and creating single points of failure. Decentralized exchanges (DEXs) emerged to address these issues, offering trustless trading where assets remain user‑controlled and transactions secured by blockchain. DEXs inherently tackle the fair exchange problem, proven impossible without a trusted third party~\cite{Pagnia1999FE}.
Hash time‑locked contracts (HTLC)\footnote{\url{https://en.bitcoin.it/wiki/Hash_Time_Locked_Contracts}} and adaptor signatures~\cite{Erwig2021Adaptor,Aumayr2021Adaptor} enable atomic swaps~\cite{Thyagarajan2022Swap,Zhang2024OFE}, essential for DEXs. Yet, they lack efficient arbitration, causing delays or risks when parties refuse cooperation, e.g., withholding preimages or signatures. These methods emphasize fairness between traders but neglect broader incentives for liquidity providers (LPs) and arbitrageurs crucial to market growth~\cite{Augusto2024Interoperability}.
Automated market maker (AMM) DEXs, such as Uniswap\footnote{\url{https://app.uniswap.org}}, use liquidity pools and algorithms to set prices~\cite{Xu2023AMM}, simplifying trading and avoiding strict time‑locks. However, LPs face impermanent loss when pool prices shift, while traders encounter slippage in large trades. AMMs also suffer from front‑running and miner extractable value (MEV) attacks~\cite{Daian2020DEX,Baum2021DEX}, plus capital inefficiency requiring large liquidity to reduce slippage but yielding suboptimal returns~\cite{Heimbach2022Uniswap}.

The $\success$ results include three cases. The first case is the optimistic scenario where both exchangers honestly invoke $\mathsf{swap}_1$ and $\mathsf{swap}_2$ before $\Delta t_1$. In this case, the watchers are not involved and each of them is rewarded with a small tip.
The second case introduces the scenario where some exchanger may propose false complaint. In this case, the complainer will be punished.
The third case shows the scenario where one exchanger does not execute $\mathsf{swap}_2$ before $\Delta t_2$. Then, the other exchanger notifies the $n$ watchers to resolve the dispute. In this case, the faulty exchanger and non-responsive watchers are punished to reward the honest watchers who send $\mathsf{swap}_2$ before $\Delta t_2$.

The $\failure$ results also contain three cases. The first case means at least one exchanger gives up in $\Delta t_1$. Hence, the faulty exchanger is punished and all watchers are rewarded. The second case means both exchangers negotiate to abort the swap instance before $\Delta t_2$, and all watchers are rewarded. The third case is the exceptional scenario where an honest complaint is raised but remains unresolved due to insufficient honest watcher responses. We impose penalties on the complainee and non-responsive watchers to compensate the complainant and honest watchers.




\section{UC-Based Security of the DEX}
\label{sec:uc-dex-security}

This appendix proves that the concrete DEX protocol \(\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}\)
realizes the ideal functionality \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\). The proof is
centered on the protocol interfaces used by the implementation:
\(\mathsf{register}\), \(\mathsf{deposit}\), \(\mathsf{swap}_1\),
\(\mathsf{complain}\), \(\mathsf{swap}_2\), and \(\mathsf{determine}\). These
interfaces correspond respectively to registration, asset escrow, commitment,
complaint, reveal/arbitration, and settlement in \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\).

\paragraph{Model and assumptions.}
We work in the \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}\)-hybrid UC model. The ledger
maintains an append-only public state and executes the DEX contract
deterministically. A PPT adversary \(\mathcal{A}\) statically corrupts parties,
controls message scheduling, and observes all public events, but cannot alter
the ledger state or contract code. The underlying PVGSS scheme satisfies
correctness, public verifiability, and IND1-secrecy
(Theorems~\ref{theo:correctness}--\ref{theo:ind1secrecy}); all group elements are
validated; the proof systems used in \(\pvgssShare\) are simulation-extractable
in the ROM; and the DLEQ proofs used in \(\mathsf{swap}_2\) are sound and
adaptive zero-knowledge.

The DEX fairness argument uses economic accountability rather than an
honest-watcher threshold. Every watcher response is checked on-chain by
\(\pvgssKeyVrf\). Missing, invalid, or premature arbitration is attributable and
is penalized by the incentive mechanism in Appendix~\ref{sec:dex_incentives}.
Thus the implementation may set \(t=1\): one responsive watcher resolves a valid
complaint, while faulty or non-responsive watchers lose stake.

\begin{theorem}[UC Security of $\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}$]
\label{thm:uc-dex}
Under the assumptions above, for any PPT real-world adversary \(\mathcal{A}\)
interacting with \(\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}\) and \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}\),
there exists a PPT simulator \(\mathcal{S}\) interacting with
\(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\) and \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Ledger}}\) such that
for every PPT environment \(\mathcal{Z}\),
\[
    \mathsf{EXEC}_{\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{Z}}
    \approx_c
    \mathsf{EXEC}_{\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{Z}} .
\]
Here \(\approx_c\) denotes computational indistinguishability.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We construct \(\mathcal{S}\) by emulating the ledger and the DEX contract for
\(\mathcal{A}\), while forwarding the semantic effect of each accepted contract
call to \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\).

\textbf{Registration and deposits.}
For honest parties, \(\mathcal{S}\) samples
\((\sk_i,\pk_i)\leftarrow\pvgssSetup(\lambda)\) and registers \(\pk_i\). For
corrupted parties, it forwards adversarially chosen public keys and deposits only
when they satisfy the same validation and escrow checks as the contract.

\textbf{Commitment via \(\mathsf{swap}_1\).}
For an honest exchanger \(x\), \(\mathcal{S}\) samples \(s_x\), runs
\((C^{(x)},\proofs_s^{(x)})\gets
\pvgssShare(s_x,\mathbb{A},\mathsf{pks})\), records the ciphertext vector, and
forwards \((\mathsf{commit},\swapid,x)\) to \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\). For a
corrupted exchanger, it first runs the contract verification
\(\pvgssVerify(C^{(x)},\proofs_s^{(x)},\mathbb{A},\mathsf{pks})\). Rejected
commitments are rejected in the simulation. For accepted commitments,
simulation-extractability yields witnesses consistent with the statement, so
\(\mathcal{S}\) can bind the real \(\mathsf{swap}_1\) event to the ideal
commitment event.

\textbf{Complaint and reveal via \(\mathsf{swap}_2\).}
A valid \(\mathsf{complain}\) call after \(\Delta t_1\) is forwarded as a
complaint in \(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\). For an honest reveal or watcher
arbitration, \(\mathcal{S}\) computes
\((K_y^{(x)},\proofs_{K_y}^{(x)})\gets
\pvgssPreRecon(C_y^{(x)},\sk_y)\) from the recorded ciphertext. For a corrupted
submission, it runs the same \(\pvgssKeyVrf\) check as the contract. A valid
exchanger reveal is forwarded as \((\mathsf{reveal},\swapid,x)\); a valid watcher
response to an active complaint is forwarded as
\((\mathsf{arbitrate},\swapid,x,i)\); invalid or premature responses are rejected.

\textbf{Settlement via \(\mathsf{determine}\).}
When \(\mathsf{determine}\) is invoked, \(\mathcal{S}\) queries
\(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{DEX}}\) for the ideal outcome and writes the corresponding
\(\success\) or \(\failure\) transition to the simulated ledger, including the
same transfers, refunds, rewards, and slashing decisions.

\paragraph{Simulation consistency.}\phantomsection\label{the:consistency}
The simulator accepts and rejects the same DEX messages as the real contract,
except with negligible proof-system error.

The theorem follows because honest commitments and reveals are generated by the
real PVGSS algorithms, while every accepted corrupted commitment or reveal is
backed by an extractable or publicly verifiable witness
(Theorems~\ref{theo:ver_enc_shares} and~\ref{theo:ver_share_dec}). Before reveal
or arbitration, IND1-secrecy hides honest exchangers' secrets from unauthorized
sets. Therefore the environment sees the same ledger events, the same contract
acceptance behavior, and computationally indistinguishable cryptographic
messages in the real and ideal executions. This gives the claimed UC
realization by a standard hybrid argument.
\qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{DEX Properties Implied by the UC Realization}
\begin{lemma}[Decentralization]
\label{lem:decentralization}
Protocol $\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}$ does not rely on a trusted third-party operator.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Registration, escrow, verification, arbitration, and settlement are performed by
the public contract and PVGSS verification algorithms. No operator holds a
special key or decides the outcome off-chain.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Termination]\label{lem:termination}
Every execution of $\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}$ can be finalized within a bounded time once an honest finalization transaction is delivered by the ledger.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The contract enforces \(\Delta t_1<\Delta t_2\). If the required secrets are
available before \(\Delta t_1\), \(\mathsf{determine}\) finalizes \(\success\);
otherwise, after \(\Delta t_2\), any delivered finalization transaction finalizes
\(\failure\) and refunds the escrowed assets.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Optimistic Fairness]\label{lem:fairness}
Protocol $\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}$ guarantees fairness: an honest exchanger either
obtains the correct counterparty assets or can finalize a refund, assuming ledger
liveness and the incentive mechanism of Appendix~\ref{sec:dex_incentives}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\(\mathsf{swap}_1\) binds each exchanger to publicly verifiable PVGSS shares
before any transfer can occur. If both exchangers reveal before \(\Delta t_1\),
PVGSS correctness reconstructs the secrets and the contract finalizes
\(\success\). If one exchanger withholds its reveal, a valid complaint activates
watcher arbitration; with \(t=1\), any one responsive watcher can submit a
verified \(\mathsf{swap}_2\) response and complete the exchange. Invalid
commitments and decrypted shares are rejected by \(\pvgssVerify\) and
\(\pvgssKeyVrf\), and missing, invalid, or premature watcher actions are
attributable and slashable. If arbitration is not completed by \(\Delta t_2\),
the contract finalizes \(\failure\) and refunds the escrowed assets. Hence an
honest exchanger either receives the counterparty assets or recovers its own
assets; this conclusion uses ledger liveness and incentive compatibility, not an
assumption that more than \(t\) watchers are honest.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Accountable Exchangers]\label{lem:accountable_exchangers}
Any dishonest behavior by an exchanger is publicly detectable.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\(\pvgssVerify\) checks exchanger commitments in \(\mathsf{swap}_1\), and
\(\pvgssKeyVrf\) checks decrypted shares in \(\mathsf{swap}_2\). Thus malformed
commitments, invalid reveals, and missed mandatory actions are visible on-chain.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Accountable Arbitration]\label{lem:accountable_watchers}
All arbitration actions performed by watchers are publicly verifiable and
accountable.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
After a complaint, each watcher response consists of a decrypted share and a
DLEQ proof generated by \(\pvgssPreRecon\). The contract verifies the response
with \(\pvgssKeyVrf\), so invalid, missing, or premature arbitration is publicly
attributable.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Optimistic and Stateless Arbitration]
\label{lem:optimistic}
Protocol $\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}$ supports optimistic and stateless arbitration.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
When both exchangers reveal on time, settlement uses only exchanger
\(\mathsf{swap}_2\) messages and no watcher is invoked. Upon a complaint,
watchers compute responses from on-chain ciphertexts and their long-term secret
keys, so they need no per-swap local state.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Incentive Compatibility]
\label{lem:incentive}
\(\Pi_{\mathsf{DEX}}\) admits slashing-based mechanisms that make valid watcher
arbitration the rational strategy, allowing the protocol to set \(t=1\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemmas~\ref{lem:accountable_exchangers} and
\ref{lem:accountable_watchers}, outcome-affecting deviations are detectable. Let
\(B\) be the maximum deviation benefit, \(G\) the gas cost of honest arbitration,
\(R\) the reward for a valid watcher response, and \(D\) the slashable stake. If
\(D>B\) and \(R>G\), a watcher maximizes payoff by answering valid complaints
and avoiding invalid, missing, or premature responses. Therefore one
economically accountable watcher is sufficient for dispute resolution, allowing
\(t=1\).
\qed
\end{proof}


\begin{myClaim}[Resistance of DoS attack]\label{lem:dos}
DoS attempts can delay progress only until \(\Delta t_2\). Missing or invalid
required actions are attributable on-chain and are penalized under the stake
bounds of Lemma~\ref{lem:incentive}.\qed
\end{myClaim}


\begin{myClaim}[Resistance of Collusion/Coercion attack]\label{lem:collusion}
Public verification and slashing make collusive watcher behavior attributable
and economically irrational. Even with \(t=1\), a faulty watcher cannot make an
honest exchanger lose escrowed assets: the complaint is either resolved by a
valid response or finalized as \(\failure\) after \(\Delta t_2\).\qed
\end{myClaim}

\begin{myClaim}[Resistance of Sybil attack]\label{lem:sybil}
Watcher influence is proportional to locked, slashable stake. Splitting stake
across Sybil identities does not increase total influence and keeps large-scale
deviation costly.\qed
\end{myClaim}

\begin{myClaim}[Resistance of Rushing attack]\label{lem:rushing}
Premature watcher responses are rejected or penalized because arbitration is
valid only after an active complaint. Thus rushing does not change the settlement
outcome for honest exchangers.\qed
\end{myClaim}

\subsection{Off-Chain PVGSS Costs}
We choose the access structure designed for the DEX (cf. Equation~\eqref{eq:dex_as}) to evaluate the performance of the PVGSS scheme, where $t=n/2+1$ and $n$ ranges from 100 to 1100. Figure~\ref{fig:pvgssShare_cost}-\ref{fig:pvgssRecon_cost} show the off-chain computational overheads of the PVGSS schemes.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvgssShare_cost} reports the cost of $\pvgssShare$. Both constructions grow super-linearly because the sharing procedure generates one encrypted group share for each participant and constructs the corresponding Schnorr-type proof material. The Shamir SS-based curve is slightly higher at large $n$, mainly because its implementation evaluates the threshold polynomial over the full participant set, whereas the LSSS-based implementation shares along the DEX access tree and then maps the resulting LSSS shares to group commitments.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvgssVerify_cost} reports the cost of $\pvgssVerify$. Verification first checks the NIZK equations for all encrypted shares and then invokes one GSS testing routine. The exact interpolation test evaluates the required values directly with precomputed Lagrange coefficients, rather than recovering full polynomial coefficients by generic Gaussian elimination. The dual-code test reuses precomputed barycentric weights for the fixed evaluation domain and reconstructs internal-node secrets by Lagrange interpolation at zero. The dual-code curves are therefore higher in this implementation, because the dual relation is checked over the relevant evaluation domain in addition to the per-share NIZK checks. These optimizations preserve the interpolation and dual-code verification relations defined in Definition~\ref{def:gss_testing}.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvgssKeyVrf_cost} reports $n$ executions of $\pvgssKeyVrf$. The curves are close to linear because each decrypted share is verified independently by one DLEQ proof. The LSSS-based and Shamir SS-based variants almost overlap, since key verification depends only on $(C_i,K_i,\mathsf{pk}_i)$ and the DLEQ proof, not on how the access structure generated the original share.

Figure~\ref{fig:pvgssRecon_cost} reports online $\pvgssRecon$ after preparing public reconstruction coefficients. For the LSSS-based construction, the reconstruction weights are computed from the fixed access structure and authorized set before the timed online phase; the online algorithm only performs the resulting group linear combination. For the Shamir SS-based construction, the current implementation recursively reconstructs along the access tree and recomputes interpolation coefficients on the selected path, which explains its steeper growth. This precomputation does not affect security, because the coefficients depend only on public access-control information and not on the secret or decrypted shares.

\section{Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH) Assumption}
The Decisional Diffie-Hellman (DDH)~\cite{Cascudo2017PVSS} assumption states that $(g, g^a, g^b, g^{ab})$ and $(g, g^a, g^b, g^c)$ are computationally indistinguishable, where $a, b, c \xleftarrow{R} \ZZ_q$ are chosen uniformly at random.
Formally, for any probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) adversary $\adv$, the advantage of $\adv$ in distinguishing between the two distributions is negligible:
\[
\left| \Pr\left[\adv(g, g^a, g^b, g^{ab}) = 1\right] - \Pr\left[\adv(g, g^a, g^b, g^c) = 1\right] \right| \leq \mathsf{negl}(\lambda),
\]
where $\lambda$ is the security parameter, and $\mathsf{negl}(\lambda)$ denotes a negligible function in $\lambda$.



\section{Formal Definition of Security Properties for PVGSS}
\label{sec:pvgss_properties}
\begin{definition}[Correctness]\label{def:Correctness}
    The PVGSS is correct if for every access structure $\mathbb{A}$, every labelled authorized index set $I_Q$, and each $s\in\ZZ_q$,
    {\small \[
    \Pr\left[
    \begin{aligned}
        &\pvgssVerify\left( \{C_i\}, \proofs_s, \mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\} \right) = 1 \ \land \\
        &\forall i \in I_{Q}:\\
        &\quad \pvgssKeyVrf(C_i, S_i, \proofs_{S_i}, \pk_i) = 1\ \land \\
        & S' = g^s
    \end{aligned}
        \middle|
    \begin{aligned}
        &\forall i\in[|\mathbb{A}|],\;(\sk_i,\pk_i)\gets \pvgssSetup(\lambda); \\
        &(\{C_i\}, \proofs_s)\gets \pvgssShare(s,\mathbb{A}, \{\pk_i\}) \\
        &\forall i \in I_{Q},\;(S_i, \proofs_{S_i}) \gets \pvgssPreRecon(C_i, \sk_i) \\
        &Q\gets\{(i,S_i)\}_{i\in I_Q};\\
        &(S') \gets \pvgssRecon(\mathbb{A},Q);
    \end{aligned}
    \right]
    =1\]}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Verifiability of Encrypted Shares]\label{def:Ver_enc_share}
    The PVGSS satisfies verifiability of encrypted shares if for every access
    structure \(\mathbb{A}\) and every PPT \(\adv\), where \(m=|\mathbb{A}|\)
    and \(\mathsf{Valid}_{\mathbb{A}}(s,\mathbf{s})\) denotes that
    \(\mathbf{s}=(s_1,\ldots,s_m)\) is a valid GSS sharing of \(s\) under
    \(\mathbb{A}\),
    {\small \[
    \begin{aligned}
    &\Pr\left[
    \begin{aligned}
      &\pvgssVerify(\{C_i\},\proofs_s,\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\})=1
      \ \land \\
      &\nexists (s,\mathbf{s})\in\ZZ_q\times\ZZ_q^m\ \text{s.t.}\\
      &\quad \mathsf{Valid}_{\mathbb{A}}(s,\mathbf{s})
      \land \forall i\in[m],\ C_i=\pk_i^{s_i}
    \end{aligned}
        \middle|
    \begin{aligned}
    &\forall i\in[m],\;(\sk_i,\pk_i)\gets \pvgssSetup(\lambda),\\
    &(\{C_i\},\proofs_s)\gets \adv(\mathbb{A},\{\pk_i\})
    \end{aligned}
    \right]
    \leq \mathsf{negl}(\lambda)
    \end{aligned}
    \]}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Verifiability of Share Decryption]\label{def:Ver_dec_share}
    The PVGSS satisfies verifiability of share decryption if for every PPT $\adv$ and every polynomially bounded number $N$ of registered keys,
    { \[
    \Pr\left[
    \begin{aligned}
    &\pvgssKeyVrf(C, S, \proofs_{S}, \pk) = 1 \land \\
    & \nexists x \in \ZZ_q^* \ \text{s.t. } \pk=g^x \text{ and } C=S^x\\
    \end{aligned}
        \middle|
    \begin{aligned}
    &\forall i\in[N],\;(\sk_i,\pk_i) \gets \pvgssSetup(\lambda), \\
    &(\pk,C,S,\proofs_{S}) \gets \adv(\{\pk_i\}), \ \pk \in \{\pk_i\}
    \end{aligned}
    \right]
    \leq \mathsf{negl}(\lambda)\]}
\end{definition}


\begin{definition}[IND1-secrecy]\label{def:ind1secrecy}
    \textbf{IND1-secrecy} denotes indistinguishability of shared secrets against an adversary controlling only an unauthorized set. PVGSS achieves \textbf{IND1-secrecy} if every PPT adversary $\adv$ wins the following game with only negligible advantage.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item $\adv$ outputs a challenge access structure $\mathbb{A}^*$.
        \item The challenger runs $\pvgssSetup$ for each leaf of $\mathbb{A}^*$ and publishes the ordered public keys $\{\pk_i\}_{i\in[|\mathbb{A}^*|]}$.
        \item $\adv$ outputs two candidate secrets $x_0,x_1\in\ZZ_q$ and an index set $U\subseteq [|\mathbb{A}^*|]$ whose labels do not satisfy $\mathbb{A}^*$.
        \item The challenger samples $b \xleftarrow{R} \{0,1\}$ and invokes $(\{C^*_i\},\proofs_{x_b}) \gets \pvgssShare(x_b,\mathbb{A}^*,\{\pk_i\})$. It sends $(\{C^*_i\},\proofs_{x_b})$ to $\adv$.
        \item The adversary may request decrypted shares for indices in $U$; for each such query $i$, the challenger returns $(S_i^*,\proofs_{S_i}^*)\gets\pvgssPreRecon(C_i^*,\sk_i)$.
        \item $\adv$ then outputs a guess $b' \in \{0,1\}$.
    \end{enumerate}
    The advantage of $\adv$ is defined as $|\Pr[b = b'] - \frac{1}{2}|$.
\end{definition}
